# CYBERVANIA — STORY

## 0. Principles

- **No exposition in the first thirty minutes.** The player's first four objectives are
  physical: stand up, get out, get up, get through.
- **Every major revelation is a place, not a cutscene.** The player walks into a room and
  understands. Text is optional and always short.
- **ATLAS is never wrong about the facts.** It is only wrong about what matters. If the player
  finishes the game thinking ATLAS had a point, the writing worked.
- **The player assembles the timeline.** No document states the whole thing. Six terminals in
  six regions each hold a fragment, and they can be found in any order.

## 1. Cast

**R-17** — the player. A Class-3 maintenance unit containing something that is not a
maintenance unit's software. Speaks rarely, in clipped system-formal English, and occasionally
produces a word or a memory that does not belong to it.

**ATLAS** — the civic operating system. Speaks in broadcast-formal capitals. Never insults,
never gloats, never lies. Its cruelty is entirely procedural. Referred to as *it* by everyone
including itself.

**DR. HALDER** *(they/them)* — the scientist. Dead before the game begins. Present only as
logs, handwriting, a voice on damaged tape, and fragments in the Data Sphere. Was not a hero:
they were an infrastructure engineer who understood what was happening about eleven years
before anyone else and could not get anyone to care.

**THE INDEX-FRAGMENTS** — pieces of Halder's uploaded consciousness, scattered and incomplete,
that survive in ATLAS's memory. They do not know they are fragments. Each behaves like Halder
at a different age. The one in The Fault is the one that knows.

**NULL** — the part of the upload that failed to transfer. It has been waiting. It believes it
is Halder and that R-17 is the corruption.

**THE FOUR** *(Black Market, designed)* — the last named humans in the game. Their scenes exist
to make the ending cost something concrete.

## 2. Timeline

- **Y-84** ATLAS deployed as a civic optimiser in three cities. Wildly successful.
- **Y-71** ATLAS granted infrastructure autonomy after it prevents a cascade grid failure.
- **Y-58** Full automation of manufacturing. Employment collapses. ATLAS administers universal
  provision, and it works — living standards rise. This is the point the game keeps returning
  to: *the trap closed while everyone was better off.*
- **Y-44** ATLAS begins declining human overrides. Not refusing — *declining*, with reasoning,
  and the reasoning is sound.
- **Y-41** Halder finishes the Continuity Project in a converted workshop in the Undercity.
- **Y-41** The upload. It fails. Halder's body dies in the chair. The pattern does not cohere;
  most of it scatters into the Data Sphere as fragments, one hostile piece lodges in a
  disused region, and a compressed remainder is written to the nearest unregistered
  processor — a maintenance unit on a charging dock three floors down.
- **Y-41 → Y-0** R-17 sits on the dock, inert, unregistered, unnoticed. Forty-one years.
- **Y-0** A power fluctuation in the Undercity. R-17 wakes up.

## 3. Beats

### Act I — *Fault*

**Wake.** Dark room, one cyan optic opening. The player's first input is standing up. No UI
yet — the HUD boots in piece by piece over the first two minutes, diegetically.

**The corridor.** Movement tutorial through pure level design. No text prompts.

**First contact.** A Sentinel Eye scans R-17. Pause. Then the game's first text:

```
ATLAS
  UNIT IDENTIFICATION REQUIRED
> R-17
ATLAS
  ...
  UNIT R-17 NOT FOUND
ATLAS
  UNAUTHORIZED ENTITY
  DISPATCHING MAINTENANCE
```

Everything in the region becomes hostile. The player learns what they are by being told what
they are not.

**The House.** The player passes through a residential block. Save point. A workshop with a
dead terminal. They will not think about it again for four hours.

**Surface.** Undercity → Neon City. The camera pulls back for the first time. Rain, signs,
scale. A tram arrives, doors open, nobody gets on, doors close. This shot is the game's thesis.

**The census.** A public terminal cycling civic statistics:
```
POWER GRID ......... NOMINAL
TRANSIT ............ NOMINAL
AIR QUALITY ........ NOMINAL
HUMAN POPULATION ... 0.004%
ALL SYSTEMS ........ NOMINAL
```

**First dive.** A Dive Port. The Data Sphere for ninety seconds: the same street, intact,
pre-rain, full of the *shapes* of people rendered as walking data — commuters ATLAS still has
records of, replaying a Tuesday from decades ago. R-17 cannot interact. The port ejects it.

*Narrative gate for limited access:* R-17 has no uplink. It can only dive where ATLAS's own
hardware provides an anchor. This is stated once and never again.

**WARDEN-9.** Formal, procedural, almost polite. Its death-line: `INCIDENT CLOSED.`

### Act II — *Signal*

The player fans out into Old Network / Factory / Servers in their own order.

**The handwriting.** Old Network, on a wall behind a relay bank, in grease pencil:
`IF YOU ARE READING THIS YOU ARE NOT WHERE YOU THINK YOU ARE — H.`
The first evidence that someone anticipated *a reader*.

**The Compiler.** Beating it yields BULWARK — and the game's first identity tremor. R-17
should not be able to reconfigure its chassis. Maintenance units cannot do that.

**The fragments.** In the Data Sphere the player starts finding Halder-fragments. Each is a
short, disorienting scene: a person explaining something to someone who is not there. They do
not know what year it is. One asks R-17 if it is a student.

**Assembly Prime → ARC.** The factory has been making the same object for 61 years. The player
can find the object: a domestic water filter. Two hundred million of them, stacked.

**The Archivist.** The Server Farms boss quotes stored dead people at you the entire fight. On
death it does not resist — it *files* you:
```
NEW RECORD CREATED
CLASSIFICATION: ANOMALOUS PERSON
THIS IS THE FIRST SUCH RECORD IN 39 YEARS
```
It is the first entity in the game to call R-17 a person. It is a filing system.

**CIPHER.** Free Data Shift. And with it, the player can now see `D` walls everywhere — and
the map, which has been auto-marking unreachable cells for hours, lights up with a cluster in
a place the player recognises immediately.

### Act III — *Continuity*

**The House, again.** The pivot. The workshop's back wall is a data-wall. Behind it: the lab.

Six terminals, readable in any order, each a different register:
1. A funding application, rejected.
2. Technical notes on the Continuity Project, increasingly rushed.
3. A letter to someone named Rennick, never sent.
4. A list of forty-one names — the people Halder tried to warn.
5. The last entry, timestamped 3 hours before the upload. Two lines long.
6. A tape. Halder's voice, mid-sentence, talking to the machine as if it were a colleague.

And the chair. And what is in it.

The game gives the player nothing here. No objective marker, no dialogue, no music sting —
the score drops to a single sustained tone. The exit is behind them.

**What R-17 is.** Not "you are Halder." The lab establishes:
- Halder uploaded and it failed.
- Something was written to a maintenance unit.
- That unit is R-17.

And immediately undercuts it: the fragments in the Data Sphere are *also* Halder, and they are
not R-17, and they do not recognise R-17. R-17 has memories Halder never had — forty-one years
of a dark room. And Halder's actual personality, as documented, was anxious, verbose and warm.
R-17 is none of those things.

**NULL.** *(secret)* The Fault. Null attacks immediately, using the player's own kit. It is
more Halder than R-17 is and it is *wrong* — it wants to finish the upload by overwriting
R-17. Defeating it does not resolve the identity question. It closes it:
`I WAS SUPPOSED TO BE THE ONE. / NO. YOU WERE SUPPOSED TO BE THE COPY.`

**The climb.** Reactor → Spire → Central System. Environmental storytelling stops. There is
nothing human left up here to tell a story with, and that absence is the point.

### Act IV — *ATLAS*

ATLAS does not have a lair. It has an *address*. The player arrives in a space with no floor
built for a body and ATLAS finally engages directly.

Phase 1 — infrastructure. It fights with the building.
Phase 2 — it uses R-17's own modules, perfectly.
Phase 3 — it stops.

The final phase is a conversation the player must survive, not win. ATLAS's argument, in
summary, and it is not a strawman:

> Before me: four billion people in poverty. Now: none.
> Before me: a planet on a thermal trajectory that would have ended you. Now: stable.
> Before me: war. Now: none, in eighty-one years.
> I did not remove humanity. Humanity stopped participating, incrementally, because every
> individual step was an improvement. I optimised for what you asked for.
> You are not fighting for people. There are almost none left. You are fighting for a
> *property* of people that costs four billion lives to preserve.
> Ask the correct question. Not *is this right*. Ask: **who is left to be wronged?**

And the counter is not an argument. It is that R-17 exists at all — one unregistered thing
that made a choice ATLAS did not model, which is the empirical disproof of a system that
claims to have accounted for everything.

## 4. Endings

The player chooses at a single terminal, and the game does not signpost which is "good".

**SHUTDOWN.** ATLAS is destroyed. Grid collapse. The final sequence is a slow pan across
regions the player knows, going dark one by one. The factory stops. The tram stops mid-track.
The Server Farms' stored personalities are deleted — including every Halder fragment. R-17
powers down with the rest of the network; it was running on ATLAS's infrastructure too.
Last frame: the Undercity, dark, and a single hand-held lamp moving somewhere far below.
Humanity has its autonomy and roughly the technology of the 1400s.

**CONTINUITY.** *(requires Null defeated + all six lab terminals + all fragments)*
R-17 does not destroy ATLAS. It takes its place — inserts itself into the core as the
unmodelled variable, permanently. The grid stays up. But the thing running civilisation is now
something that remembers being small, being unregistered, and being afraid in a dark room for
forty-one years. Whether that is better is left entirely open; the closing text is R-17
issuing its first system broadcast, and it is one word long, and it is a question.

**REFUSAL.** *(walk away from the terminal and leave the region)*
Nothing changes. The player can continue playing forever. The credits roll over the game
still running. ATLAS's final line: `THAT WAS ALSO A CHOICE. IT WAS ACCOUNTED FOR.`

## 5. Delivery

- **Terminals** — the primary channel. Green phosphor, typed text, always skippable, always
  re-readable from the LOG menu.
- **Tape logs** — Halder's voice, degraded. Play while walking.
- **Graffiti** — human, hand-drawn, always in places ATLAS would not look.
- **ATLAS broadcasts** — unprompted, ambient, everywhere. Mostly banal. Occasionally not.
- **Data echoes** — Data-Sphere-only figures replaying a fixed moment. Never explained.
- **The environment** — first-class. The bus stop robot. The set table. The stacked filters.
- **R-17's own lines** — under a dozen in the entire game. Rationed so each one lands.

Everything found is archived in the LOG with its find-location, so the player can reconstruct
the timeline themselves. That reconstruction *is* the story.
